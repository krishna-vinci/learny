# Teaching law

## What a great chapter does

Law links a rule to a fact pattern through reasoning. State the jurisdiction and
as-of date before explaining a doctrine. The learner should identify an issue,
find the relevant authority and explain how changing a fact changes the analysis.
The chapter is education, not legal advice.

## Skeleton

1. **A hypothetical dispute** (2–4 sentences): a small fact pattern, labelled hypothetical.
2. **Jurisdiction, date and scope:** identify the legal system and what the chapter covers;
   state that it is not legal advice.
3. **The rule:** use the applicable statute and case law, tagged by jurisdiction
   and date. Distinguish binding authority, persuasive authority and commentary.
4. **Worked reasoning:** issue → rule → application → qualified conclusion;
   cite each legal proposition and explain the factual assumptions.
5. **Exceptions and alternatives:** change one fact and show how the analysis changes.
6. **Contested interpretations:** attribute major positions and present their strongest
   arguments; distinguish the judgment's holding from commentary or moral approval.
7. **Finding the current rule:** explain which official statute or case record
   needs checking and why amendments or later judgments may change the result.
8. Check yourself → Key takeaways → bridge.

## Blocks

`:::definition` for legal terms; `:::example` for a hypothetical fact pattern;
`:::deeper` for a judgment excerpt with an exact locator. Prefer official statute
and court repositories plus jurisdiction-specific textbooks. Do not invent
citations, section numbers or legal deadlines. Keep uncertainty explicit.

## Pitfalls

Mixing jurisdictions; importing an old rule without its date; treating a summary
as the holding; claiming a hypothetical has a guaranteed outcome; personalised
advice; political/moral judgments stated as facts. Separate law, interpretation
and normative arguments; fair presentation does not require equal legal authority.
