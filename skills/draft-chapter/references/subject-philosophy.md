# Teaching philosophy

## What a great chapter does

Philosophy asks what follows from reasons we can inspect. Start with a dilemma
or ordinary experience, then help the learner reconstruct an argument, challenge
its premises and explain a rival position fairly. Understanding a school is not
agreement with it.

## Skeleton

1. **A dilemma** (2–4 sentences): one concrete choice that makes the question matter.
2. **The question and vocabulary:** define only the terms needed to follow the argument.
3. **The strongest argument:** lay out premises and conclusion in plain language;
   distinguish a valid inference from a true premise.
4. **A worked case:** apply the position to the opening dilemma and show the reasoning.
5. **Major rival positions:** present each relevant school's strongest argument,
   with named attribution and primary-text or scholarly-survey citations.
6. **Objections and replies:** separate what an author wrote from a later interpretation;
   explain where the disagreement actually lies, without declaring a moral winner.
7. **What remains open:** identify a premise or question the learner can investigate.
8. Check yourself → Key takeaways → bridge.

## Blocks

`:::definition` for technical terms. `:::example` for an argument reconstructed
step by step. `:::deeper` for a primary-text passage or scholarly dispute.
Use a table only when it clarifies premises or competing answers, not to flatten
schools into slogans. Prefer Stanford/Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy,
primary texts on Wikisource/Gutenberg and PhilPapers survey articles.

## Pitfalls

Straw-manning a rival; treating an interpretation as a settled fact; presenting
political or moral judgments as facts; confusing ancient and contemporary senses
of a term; invented quotations; using a quote instead of explaining the argument.
Attribute positions, separate facts from interpretations and date changing claims.

## Model excerpt (style only — never reuse its facts or numbers)

> You can control the promise you make; can you control whether it succeeds?
> Before answering, separate the action from its outcome. One position makes
> the quality of your choice central; another asks whether a good choice can
> ignore its effects on other people.[^src:…] We will give both arguments their
> strongest form before testing them on the same case.
