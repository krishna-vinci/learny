# Teaching politics

## What a great chapter does

Politics concerns institutions, power and choices people contest. Begin with a
public decision, then show who can do what, under which rules, and how we know.
The learner should distinguish an institutional fact from an actor's claim or
an interpretation of its consequences.

## Skeleton

1. **A public decision** (2–4 sentences): a bill, budget or vote with an explicit date.
2. **The institutional question:** identify the jurisdiction, period and participants.
3. **Rules and mechanisms:** explain the relevant constitution, parliamentary
   procedure or official record, with a worked journey from proposal to outcome.
4. **Evidence:** distinguish primary documents, dated official statistics and
   attributed testimony. Explain the limits of each.
5. **Perspectives:** present major relevant positions fairly with their strongest
   arguments; name speakers or schools and separate facts from interpretations.
6. **A comparison:** test competing explanations against the same documented case.
7. **What can change:** date every claim, state the record's cutoff and identify
   which rules or officeholders would need checking again.
8. Check yourself → Key takeaways → bridge.

## Blocks

`:::definition` for institutional vocabulary; `:::example` for following a bill
or interpreting a parliamentary record; `:::deeper` for an unresolved debate.
Prefer constitutions, parliamentary records and official statistics, plus
multiple attributed scholarly perspectives. A diagram may show a documented
procedure; it must not imply an inevitable political outcome.

## Pitfalls

A single partisan outlet as sole evidence; undated claims; opinion disguised as
fact; invented motives; confusing formal powers with political practice;
false equivalence between claims with different evidence. Never present a
political or moral judgment as fact. Date claims and distinguish the source's
words, documented events and your explanation.

## Model excerpt (style only — never reuse its facts or numbers)

> A proposal reaches the chamber, but it has not yet become a rule. Follow
> the recorded steps and ask who may amend, delay or reject it.[^src:…]
> Supporters describe the proposal one way; critics describe it another.
> We can attribute those judgments while checking the procedure against the
> same dated parliamentary record.
